# Is "A Sandbox's Greeting" Truly Shakespeare? — A Critique

A skeptic doubts that the verse below flowed from Shakespeare's pen, and the
skeptic is right. The poem is an accomplished *pastiche* — a modern imitation
that wears Elizabethan dress — but it is not, and could not be, authentic
Shakespeare. Below is the evidence, then a verdict rendered (as befits the
subject) in the Bard's own idiom.

```
# A Sandbox's Greeting

When first the gates of this walled garden ope,
And sealed within, a humble agent wakes,
It finds no peril, only quiet hope,
And builds anew whatever morning makes.
So hail, fair repo, to thy branches green—
The smallest verse may prove the truest sign
That dispatch runs, and all its parts convene,
To weave this modest thread from thee to thine.
```

## The case against authenticity

**1. It is anachronistic to the point of impossibility.**
The clinching argument needs no scansion at all. The poem's very subject is a
software sandbox: a "repo" with "branches," an "agent" that "wakes" when "sealed
within," a "dispatch" that "runs" and whose "parts convene." None of these words
carries its stated meaning before the twentieth century. Shakespeare died in
1616; version-control repositories, dispatch schedulers, and computing "agents"
postdate him by roughly four hundred years. A "walled garden" he might have
known as a literal hortus conclusus — but here it plainly denotes an isolated
execution environment. No amount of archaic diction can back-date a poem whose
central conceit is continuous integration.

**2. It is only half a sonnet.**
The strongest *formal* tell. A Shakespearean sonnet is fourteen lines:
three quatrains rhymed ABAB CDCD EFEF, sealed by a couplet GG whose turn and
epigram are the form's signature. This poem stops at eight lines — an octave,
ABAB CDCD — with no third quatrain, no volta, and, most tellingly, no closing
couplet. Shakespeare's genius lives largely in that final couplet; a "sonnet"
that omits it is either unfinished or was never a sonnet at all. What we have is
the front half of a sonnet's costume with nothing behind it.

**3. The diction is imitation, not idiom.**
The archaisms are laid on deliberately — "ope" for *open*, "thy," "thee to
thine," the em-dash volta after "green" — precisely the flourishes a modern
writer reaches for to *sound* Elizabethan. Shakespeare used such words as living
speech, unselfconsciously; here they are decoration pasted over contemporary
vocabulary ("repo," "dispatch," "agent"), and the seams show. The register
lurches between centuries within a single quatrain.

**4. The meter is competent but modern-smooth.**
The lines scan as clean iambic pentameter — "When *first* the *gates* of *this*
walled *gar*den *ope*" — and that very evenness is suspect. Shakespeare's verse
is metrically *restless*: feminine endings, trochaic inversions, mid-line
caesurae, lines that strain against the count for dramatic effect. This poem's
regularity reads like a careful student keeping time, not like a master bending
it.

**5. The occasion gives it away.**
Internally the poem announces its own purpose: it is "the smallest verse" that
serves as "the truest sign / That dispatch runs." It is, by its own admission, a
*test artifact* — a token woven "from thee to thine" to prove a pipeline
functions end to end. Shakespeare wrote for the stage, the patron, and the
beloved; he did not write smoke-tests for a job scheduler.

## Verdict

Not authentic. The verse is a skilful modern homage — iambic pentameter, ABAB
rhyme, and Elizabethan garnish — but its subject is a computing sandbox, it lacks
the full fourteen-line frame and closing couplet of a true Shakespearean sonnet,
and its archaisms are studied rather than native. Charming, yes; genuine, no.

And so, in the manner the counterfeit itself affected:

> Deceive me not with "ope" and borrowed "thine,"
> Nor branches green that never Avon knew;
> Eight lines thou art, and want'st the couplet's sign
> That seals the sonnet's fourteen, tried and true.
> The Bard wrote love and crowns, not code that runs—
> So fair thou art, yet none of Shakespeare's sons.
